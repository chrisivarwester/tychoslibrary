2.1 Sources and method

No catalogue of Tycho Brahe's library is known.1 The one record of the library as a whole is a line in the meteorological diary kept at Uraniborg: under 21 March 1597, three days after the royal pension stopped and in the last weeks of the residence on Hven, an assistant wrote Registeret vi alle junkers bøger, ‘we registered all the junker's books’.2 The register itself has not been found.1 Norlind took the entry to show that no catalogue had existed before;3 Gingerich allowed that the line could refer to the stock of Tycho's own printed books as well as to the library, but read it as a count of the library.4 Among the Brahe papers in Vienna, Friis noted in 1868 ‘a list of Tycho Brahe's books’ and a second list beginning Ein verdorben Exemplar, ‘a spoilt copy’ (Cod. 10,688); Dreyer described the three leaves (foll. 9–11) as a count of the copies of a book still in stock and printed their figures for the Progymnasmata and De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis, and Mortensen, citing Norlind, suggested that the list might be an inventory of the unsold stock of Tycho's own works.5

The library is therefore reconstructed from two directions, as Norlind set out in 1970: from the volumes that survive with Tycho's marks, and from the books he names as his own, or can be shown to have owned, in his letters and printed works.6 The first direction was opened for Prague by Friis (1868–69), Studnička (1901), Prusík (1901), Kukula (1906–07), Prandtl (1932) and Kleinschnitzová (1933), and for the volumes outside Prague by Prandtl (1932) and Mortensen (1955); Gigas (1900) and Mortensen (1931) described the marks by which the volumes are recognised. The second direction was opened by Norlind (1926, 1970). The sequence is set out under The identification of the surviving volumes. The catalogue rests on both directions: it has 251 entries, the works attested as Tycho's at any grade together with the 4 disputed identifications and the 8 works bound among his but never his; 131 works are located as Tycho's own in 72 surviving volumes: 52 in the National Library of the Czech Republic, the rest in 13 other collections and in private hands.

2.2 The student's books, 1560–1570

Tycho entered the University of Copenhagen in April 1559, aged twelve.7 Gassendi's life of 1654 made the partial solar eclipse of 21 August 1560, which fell at the predicted time, the beginning of his astronomy; in Dreyer's account he ‘lost no time in procuring a copy of the Ephemerides of Stadius’.8

The earliest books are known from their bindings and purchase notes. A brown calf volume stamped Tycho Brade on the front board and Anno 1561 on the back holds Regiomontanus's Tabulae directionum (Tübingen 1550) and Apianus's Cosmographia in Gemma Frisius's recension (Paris 1553), with the note emptus thalero & 4 Gross and Tycho's name three times on the fly-title and its verso (the volume of 1561).9 Prandtl recorded it in 1932 as formerly in the stock of Antiquariat Weiss & Co. in Munich and lately sold to America;10 in fact Maggs Bros. of London offered it in 1933 at £125, the figure still pencilled on its pastedown, and again in 1934 and about 1938, and it reached New York only in Herbert Reichner's first catalogue of 1939, Reichner having been Prandtl's own publisher in Vienna.11,12 Its owner from about 1940 is unknown. It returned to Denmark by the bequest of Oscar Ekman — a Copenhagen engineer, who with his Hamburg-born wife Lia was a long-standing member of the museum's friends' society — entered by his widow in January 1957 at the Handels- og Søfartsmuseet på Kronborg, and when the Ekmans' library was sold in November 1965 the catalogue listed it for completeness only, ‘not for sale’, on view in a showcase at Kronborg;13 it was deposited by the museum at the Royal Library in 1986, lent from there to the Ole Rømer Museum's exhibition of 2001, and has since returned to the museum's keeping, where it was examined leaf by leaf in October 2026.14,15,16,9 Norlind lists the purchases of 1560 — Bayrus's medical handbook, Macer's De herbarum virtutibus and the Sacrobosco used for teaching at Copenhagen, bound together in the blind-stamped pigskin lettered T. B. 1560 — those of 1561, and a Garcaeus of 1563.17 A Sammelband of three prints of 1556–58 — Camerarius's Hippocomicus with his Latin Xenophon (Leipzig 1556), Osius's Historia excidii Hierosolymorum (Wittenberg 1558) and Garcaeus's manual of horoscopy (Wittenberg 1556) — stamped Ty Brad 1563, carries at the head of the first title the name of Anders Sørensen Vedel, Tycho's tutor at Leipzig: Walde and Mortensen took it for Tycho's gift to Vedel, Norlind for Vedel's book given to Tycho, who had it bound; it is at Uppsala.18,19,20 The facsimile shows the Garcaeus worked through, after the binding of 1563, by a reader whose hand has not been identified — subject headings in the margins of the calendar chapters, the solar cycle for 1555, the book's own example, computed by long division, the Elector August's nativity and its revolution for 1556 checked against Reinhold's tables.21

The Ptolemy of Basel 1551 in Prague (Sign. 5 B 70) carries at the foot of its title page the note Emptus Haffniae 2 Joachimicis 1560 ultimo Novembris, ‘bought at Copenhagen for two Joachimsthaler on the last day of November 1560’. Friis, surveying the Prague remains in 1868, read the note as written by Tycho himself and the marginalia as his;22 Dreyer in 1890 made the Ptolemy the student's first step beyond the Stadius, his resolve ‘to go to the fountain-head’;23 Studnička and Prandtl followed him, Prandtl adding that it carries many marginal notes and calculations in Tycho's hand.24,25 The same title page carries the name of Johannes Pratensis (1543–1576), Tycho's friend and later professor of medicine at Copenhagen, and the front board is blind-stamped I. P. 1561. Kleinschnitzová observed in 1933 that a binding stamped for Pratensis in 1561 contradicts a purchase by Tycho in 1560 — Studnička had explained the date as the binder's error — and supposed that the book came to Tycho later, perhaps only after Pratensis's death in 1576;26 the National Library's catalogue of 2024 reads Pratensis as the first owner and Tycho as the second, probably from his estate.27 Christianson in 1967 took the volume for Pratensis's, ‘from whom Tycho probably inherited it in 1576’, and printed a note in Dreyer's hand (Royal Library, NKS 3077 4°, p. 101) recording Friis's opinion, against his own print of 1868, that the notes are few and insignificant and were made by Pratensis.28 Every account since Friis's counts the volume as Tycho's; the evidence is its place in the donation of 1642, whose inscription it carries, and the marginalia that Studnička listed page by page as Tycho's.29 Neither settles the matter alone: the inscription is the college's label for the donation and stands also on Kepler's De stella nova of 1606 (below),30,31 and a comparison of the hands on the facsimile, made on 25 October 2026, settled nothing: the purchase note agrees with the Pratensis signatures and with Tycho's dated specimens only in the common forms of the period and differs from each in one or two constructions — from the signatures in the h and the minims, from Tycho's hand in the capital I and the final s — and the marginalia draw on a German-cursive repertoire, the 3-shaped final s, the open e, the final i with a descender, that Tycho's secure hands show nowhere, while sharing his u-marks.32 On the evidence as it stands the book was Pratensis's — bought on 30 November 1560, bound for him in 1561 — and Tycho's only later, not before 1561 and probably after Pratensis's death in 1576: Dreyer's reading of the note as Tycho's own, repeated by Prusík, Kukula, Prandtl and Šolc in 1999 without a comparison, has no positive support, and what makes the volume Tycho's at all is the college's note of 1642 and its place in the donation from his heirs. Tycho remembered Pratensis in 1584 in an epitaph printed on his own press, which Nielsen took, with reservation, for the earliest print known from it.33,34 On the present evidence one surviving volume is documented as Tycho's before he left Denmark on 14 February 1562: the volume of 1561, its boards stamped with his name and the year, the earliest purchase note standing on its fly-title beside an ownership entry in his name. The pigskin volume stamped T. B. 1560 (18 J 318) is attributed on that cipher alone, without inscription or annotation, and its Jesuit inscription is the professed house's, not the college's of 1642 — Kleinschnitzová, who listed it, could only say it had strayed there — so it is graded C; the bindings of 1563 and 1567 belong to the Leipzig and Rostock years. Christianson's ‘two of his own “textbooks”’ are thus one volume and a candidate; the ephemerides with which, by his own account, he began have not survived.35

From 1562 to 1565 Tycho studied at Leipzig under Vedel's supervision. Dreyer records that he spent most of his money on astronomical books and instruments, having to account to his tutor for it, that he used the Ephemerides of Stadius to find the places of the planets, and that he soon added the Alphonsine and Prutenic tables.36 After some months at home he left early in 1566 for Wittenberg and, when plague broke out there, for Rostock.37 The Euclid of Basel 1558 at Strahov (AV II 2) has TYCHO / BRAHE and the year 1567 blind-stamped on its front board, the only dated name-stamp in the spelling Brahe; it carries, on one opening, marginal notes which Pařez attributes to Tycho and whose hand has not been established as his, and no Jesuit inscription.38 In 1568–70 Tycho travelled through Germany to Basel and Augsburg, where he stayed more than a year;39 Norlind observes that long before Hven he had sought out the astronomical literature his studies required, and that the journey of 1575, which took in the Frankfurt fair, was probably especially productive for the library.40 Westman writes that on that journey, which took him through southern Germany, Basel and Venice, Tycho ‘bought more than thirty books for his library’ and ‘embossed his seal on a substantial number of books’; the Naibod of 1573 is among those that survive, his De revolutionibus is not.41

2.3 Uraniborg: the room, the bindery and the dated bindings, 1576–1597

Frederik II granted Tycho the island of Hven by a letter of 23 May 1576, and the foundation stone of Uraniborg was laid on 8 August.42,43 The library occupied the southern round room of the ground floor, which Tycho called the Museum; the great brass globe stood in it, the chemical laboratory with its furnaces lay in the cellar beneath, and the southern observatory above.44,45,46 Tycho himself placed it: the Museum where his students worked, ‘where his library is also found’, lay ‘in media contignatione rotundæ Turriculæ versus Austrum’, on the middle storey of the round south turret — the house’s main floor, the turret’s three levels being the laboratory below, the museum and the observing room above (the Synopsis of the instruments printed in the Epistolae of 1596; the German description that went to the Landgrave with the letter of 26 September 1591, compiled by Tycho's amanuensis, says the same).47,48 Christianson describes the desks with quills, ink and manuscript handbooks of prosthaphaeresis, and the room as the working centre of the house;46 Tycho's own account gives four tables for the six, eight or at times twelve students who computed there, a column in the middle of the round room with the great globe behind it, six or eight other globes, smaller instruments and automata, the painted boards between the windows and a porphyry stone with Plato's dictum in gold, and the engraving of 1598 shows a part of the library as two open shelves with the books standing upright;49 Mortensen, who gives it a diameter of five metres, notes that the globe, smaller instruments, automata, pictures and inscriptions shared it, so that the space for books was limited.50 The room's measure, and what its walls could hold, is set out under the size of the library below. Norlind supposes that certain tables and handbooks were in permanent use in the underground rooms of Stjerneborg, and that the letter archive, growing with the years, stood with the books.51

Nielsen and Prandtl place a bindery on the island beside the press and the paper mill. Nielsen: ‘besides the printing house he established both a bindery and a paper mill, so that in the end he became almost completely independent of the outside world in all his various undertakings’;52 Prandtl states that the binder bound ‘not only the books he published himself but also those bought for his library, and furnished them with Tycho’s stamps’.53 Neither dates the bindery. The press was announced in a letter to Vedel of 16 February 1584 and was printing by the end of that year: Tycho noted in his observation journal that on 27 November 1584 he had himself printed on it, ‘as the very first thing’, a poem to Erik Lange, of which no printed copy survives, the text being known from a transcript; the epitaph on Pratensis, signed at Uraniborg in 1584, is in Nielsen’s judgement probably the oldest of the poems printed late in 1584 and early in 1585, and in that case the earliest Uraniborg imprint of which a copy is known; and Morsing’s calendar for 1586, printed late in 1585, was the first book.54,34 The paper mill, according to the inscribed stone Tycho set on its dam, was begun in 1590 and completed in 1592; it was demolished in 1602.55 Gigas, who described the presentation bindings of the Royal Library in 1900, counted two library books in Prague — the Ramus and the Maestlin–Clavius volume — among the products of the same workshop.56 He also found on the star catalogue of 1598 dedicated to Johan Adolf (GKS 307) the leaf-roll of the Epistolae of 1596 and two rosette punches that recur on a couple of the other Royal Library bindings, and described GKS 1825, Tycho’s observations ‘fra Vintersolhverv 1577 og gennem flere paafølgende Aar’, in white parchment with the portrait and the arms in gilt, the arms set the wrong way up — a protocol written before the dies existed, and so bound, or rebound, after they came into use;57 Roos, surveying the Danish armorial and portrait bindings in 1948, judged the Tycho bindings ‘mere italiensk-franske end tyske’ in style, German only in the moral inscription.58 The earliest bindings that can be placed on the island are the presentation copies of 1588 described below, which Mortensen records as printed and bound at Uraniborg;59 the dated bindings of 1576–1582 are, on Kleinschnitzová’s comparison with Vedel’s, presumably of Copenhagen origin.60

The dated bindings give a terminus for the acquisitions: Norlind takes the years, which come from Tycho, as the year of the binding and an indicator of when the works bound in it entered the library.61 Kleinschnitzová describes the type Tycho used ‘since the foundation of Uraniborg (1576)’: brown calf, a narrow gilt border, an arabesque of rhomboid form in the centre, his initials T. B. O. above it and the year below — ‘mostly 1576, but also 1580 and 1582’ — with two centre stamps, six border rolls and seven corner stamps in use.62 Šolc's account of 1999 adds three figures of its own: Tycho had up to six smaller prints of one format bound into a Sammelband; the brown bindings carry four small stars in the corners; and seven of the Clementinum volumes are in white parchment, some richly decorated and with gilt centre stamps — the uniform binding he dates to 1576, when the building of Uraniborg began, the greater part of the Uraniborg library being, in his account, of the first years after 1576 (Šolc 1999, §§ 2.529–2.530, printed p. 150).63 Prandtl notes that Tycho bought a large number of books when furnishing Uraniborg in 1576, and Norlind connects the 1576 cohort with the purchases at the Frankfurt fair of 1575.64,65 Kleinschnitzová compared these bindings with those of Vedel's library, which used the same rhomboid arabesque and similar tools until Vedel moved from Copenhagen to Ribe in 1581, and held a Copenhagen origin of the bindings admissible — at the least both men drew their tools from one source — leaving undecided which of the two was the other's model; an Albategnius bound in 1580 (14 J 28) is bound exactly like a Vedel volume of the same year.60 The titles of the works were stamped on the bindings with letter punches. On the Commandino–Pitati–Galesio volume of 1576 (5 H 65) they stand on the edge of the text block in blind capitals, one line to a work — COMPENDIVM PITATI, GALESIVS DE TERRAE MOTV, the first line worn away — and the library's record of the Proclus volume of the same year (5 J 2) notes a chiselled title on its edge; on the Albategnius of 1580 the title ALBATEGNIVS is stamped across the board beneath the initials.66 Studnička wrote in 1901 that Tycho united works of a kind in composite volumes ‘with the titles impressed in the elegant gilt edge’, and Kleinschnitzová that Vedel had the titles of his composite volumes impressed in the edge and that the Tychoniana were furnished in the same way; the lettering on the edge of 5 H 65 is blind, not gilt, and the gilt gauffered edges of the later plaque bindings carry no lettering.67,60 A title on the edge is read from a book standing with its edge outward; the engraving of 1598 shows the volumes upright and does not show which face was turned to the room.49 One Vedel book stands among the Prague Tychoniana: Glareanus's De geographia (Freiburg 1536), inscribed Sum And: Seuer. Wellej Danj 1564. Mensis Aug. Die 26 (Sign. 19 G 20).68 A cost note on the first leaf of the Sacrobosco manuscript once bound before the astrological Sammelband (VII E 29, 14 F 16) lists a Glareanus at 21 pfennigs before the Gauricus, the Albubater and the Cardanus of that volume and the binder's fee; it is headed with the name of Andreas Schöner, whose nativity stands on the leaves bound at the end of the Albubater. Kleinschnitzová set beside it the binder’s instruction on the last leaf of the Glareanus, in her judgement Tycho’s hand — poni anti hos I Comentatorium Alcabitij II Fragmentum illud Astrologicum deinde III Tractatulus Gauricij cum montulmo IIII Albubater arabicus Astrologus — and concluded that the Glareanus of the cost note is this volume, once bound in the Sammelband with the Gauricus and the Albubater, whose spine signature attests the Chomutov provenance inscribed on the Glareanus as well; the hands of the two notes have not been compared.69

From 1588 the gilt portrait and arms supralibros appear, ringed by the two distichs, printed among Tycho's poems in the Opera omnia and attributed to him by Zeeberg;70 Mortensen derives the portrait stamp from the engraving dated 1586, and the earliest dated bindings to carry the pair are two presentation copies of De mundi aetherei recentioribus phaenomenis printed and bound at Uraniborg in 1588, one of them inscribed to Gellius Sascerides on 20 March 1588.59,71 The portrait stamp was described and engraved in Danske Magazin in 1746, from the blue-satin Mechanica of 1598 at the Royal Library that Tycho had given to a Bohemian nobleman on 18 December 1600; the description already read the three chains, with Frederick II's portrait and the letters F. S. among them.72 Tycho used the plaques on his own books as well as on gifts: Kleinschnitzová counts six such volumes among the Prague Tychoniana and supposes that he marked in this way works ‘of particular value to him for personal or practical reasons’, and Prandtl records that he had library books bound so ‘even in the Prague period’.73,74 The dies went with him to Prague, as Studnička concluded from the Reinhold he bought there in June 1601 (14 J 176), a white-leather binding with the plaques; the binding of the Opus palatinum at Strahov, which carries them on parchment, is attributed to a Prague workshop of the Rudolfine circle and dated 1599–1601 by comparison with it.75,76,77 The sequence of marks is set out under the ownership marks.

2.4 How the books came: fairs, agents, booksellers and gifts

Mosley reconstructed from the correspondence how Tycho acquired books. Uraniborg, he writes, ‘was a net importer of books, and Tycho's correspondents seem to have been particularly prominent among its suppliers’; books and letters travelled together.78 Evidence of commercial purchase is scant. In May 1579 Tycho sent Vedel a copy of the current Frankfurt fair catalogue, to be passed to a certain Balthazar, in Mosley's reading a Copenhagen bookdealer, who was to obtain the titles Tycho had marked in the margin, ‘nine of them in Latin, three in German, and one in French’.79 Norlind holds that the accession on Hven ran mainly through purchases from the Copenhagen booksellers — at first chiefly Johannes Ålborg — and from merchants who visited the German fairs.80 In 1598 Tycho told Magini that he had had the Tabula tetragonica of 1592, a work dedicated to himself, for at least five years: a year or two after publication one of his students had brought to Uraniborg a copy that a Copenhagen bookseller had carried from the Frankfurt fair, and no copy had come from the author.81 Purchase notes survive in a few volumes: in Christmann's Observationum solarium libri tres (Basel 1601) Tycho wrote emi Pragae mense Maio (14 J 164/adl. 1), and in Reinhold's Prutenicae tabulae of 1551, the author's dedication copy to the Counts of Mansfeld, est manus Reinholdi. emi Pragae anno 1601 mense Junio (14 J 176) — four months before his death.82,83 Thoren holds that after a messenger absconded with the books he had been paid to buy, Tycho bought only through friends and associates.84

The Frankfurt fair catalogues were the common reference. In December 1578 Brucaeus at Rostock had noticed Maestlin's treatise on the comet of 1577 in the catalogue and offered to send it;85 in 1584 Johannes Maior, who had just dispatched to Tycho's order a number of texts on the reform of the calendar, noted other works on clocks and astrology in the Willer catalogues, an offer Tycho declined;86 in 1585 Brucaeus reported that no mathematical works had appeared at the recent fairs.87 Cort Aslakssøn sent a catalogue of recent authors to Hven in October 1594, offering to buy at the next Frankfurt fair whatever his former master wanted from it.88 In March 1598 Tycho, at Wandsbek, asked Longomontanus for Everaert's Ephemerides of 1597, seen in a catalogue but not to be had in Hamburg; he had a copy by May.89 Herwart von Hohenburg, the Bavarian chancellor, asked in 1599 to buy six works from the autumn catalogue, sent the catalogues of both fairs of 1600 with the same offer.90 Orders placed with more than one agent produced duplicates: Maestlin's treatise came again in 1579 from Paul Hainzel in Augsburg, with tracts by Cornelius Gemma, Röslin and others, and in 1584 both Maior and Brucaeus sent Scaliger's De emendatione temporum of 1583.91 One of the two copies survives in Prague (Sign. 19 A 46), initialled T. B. on the title page; Kleinschnitzová could not say whether it was Brucaeus's or Maior's.92

Authors sent their books. Rantzau sent his Calendarium (1590), and Mosley counts his Catalogus of astrologers (1584) among the gifts as well; Bruno's Camoeracensis acrotismus (1588) and Scaliger's Manilius (1600) reached the library the same way; the second edition of Scaliger's De emendatione temporum (1598), which Tycho acknowledged to the author from Wandsbek on 14 March 1598 as just received, Mosley counts with them, as Norlind had in 1926, whereas Walde, noting that the letter returns no thanks for a gift, took the copy for one procured in the ordinary way, and Norlind in 1970 agreed; Pitiscus presented his Trigonometria (1595) through Aslakssøn, and Monaw stopped short of giving his Symbolum (1595). Kepler, supposing that Tycho already owned his Mysterium cosmographicum (1596), sent no copy with his first letter, and Tycho replied that he had read the book before; Mosley nonetheless counts it among the authors' gifts.93 Two of these survive with inscriptions: the Rantzau, whose dedication of 1584 is the author's to Henrik Ramel, who, Norlind supposes, passed the copy to Tycho;94 and the Bruno, with Bruno's autograph dedication and, on the last page, the epigram Nullanus, nullus et nihil, / Conveniunt rebus nomina saepe suis, which Šolc gives to Tycho's hand and Šíma and Valeška, after a handwriting analysis, leave open;95 a third author's gift, Scaliger's Cyclometrica elementa duo (1594), is known as such from its inscription alone, Viro illustri atque eruditissimo Domino Tychoni Brahe, D[omi]no in Knudstrup et Vraniburgo Josephus Scaliger Jul. Caes. F. D. D. (14 A 59).96 Dedications from others than the author also occur: the Alphonsine Tables of 1492 now in Vienna carry a dedication to Tycho dated 1590 from Lave Urne, judge in Skåne, and many notes in Tycho's hand.97

Tycho's requests to correspondents turned on the literature of the new star of 1572 and of the comets, which he meant to review in his books. In 1581, having just received Hagecius's Apodixis from Scultetus, he asked for Dudith's De cometarum significatione or similar works;98 the Dudith is in the comet Sammelband 14 H 192. He pressed Hagecius for such texts from 1585 to 1590, asking him to have Italian, Spanish and French writings on the nova translated into Latin;99 Hagecius possessed Muñoz's Libro del nuevo cometa but could find no translator, so that Tycho worked from a copy of Muñoz's letter to Hagecius comparing their results and from the account in Cornelius Gemma's De naturae divinis characterismis (Antwerp 1575) — his annotated copy of the Gemma survives, and Gingerich reports that Tycho annotated the section on Muñoz heavily and quoted the passage in the Progymnasmata.100,101 In 1594 Aslakssøn sent, unasked, Clavius's commentary on Sacrobosco for the tract on the new star inserted in it.102 One book was sought for years: Werner's De motu octavae sphaerae (Nuremberg 1522), which Tycho asked Hagecius for in August 1585 — ‘for a long time I have searched far and wide’ — and obtained only in November 1588, when Hagecius sent him the copy he had had from Paul Fabricius in Vienna; a second copy, which Magini gave a student to forward in September 1590, never arrived — in December Tycho wrote that it must have been lost on the way. The Werner of 1522 in the Clementinum Sammelband 5 G 42, with his marginalia, is probably the Hagecius copy.103,104

2.5 Manuscripts, other men's libraries and the books of Paul Wittich

Manuscript copying supplied what print could not. In July 1586 Tycho told Hagecius that he would have his manuscript of George of Trebizond on Ptolemy’s Syntaxis transcribed while he sounded Plantin about printing it, ostensibly against loss of the original;105 Brucaeus offered to copy texts for him in 1584, and Hagecius was asked to do so in 1590, where printed copies had run out;106 in May 1576 Brucaeus returned to Tycho a section of Homelius’s unpublished Gnomonica that he had borrowed piecemeal, apparently to copy it, a manuscript Tycho charged Vedel in 1579 to return to Joachim Camerarius at Nuremberg.107 Vedel travelled that year with letters of introduction for the ‘scouring’ of German libraries and with Tycho’s instruction to buy ‘any books in Germany, whether about the comet, apart from those I already have, or something noteworthy on astronomical matters’.108

Tycho also asked for catalogues. In January 1585 he requested from Rantzau a list of the astrological books at Breitenburg, a library of more than six thousand volumes at its fullest;109 in March 1592 he asked Hugo Blotius for the authors and titles of the philosophical, mathematical, astronomical, medical and chemical books among the ‘more than 90,000 volumes’ of the imperial library in Vienna, and for guides to the libraries of Archduke Maximilian and others.110 In Bohemia he obtained from Herwart von Hohenburg a catalogue of the mathematical books of the Bavarian ducal library, from which, ‘although very many of them are pleasing, I have selected only fifty’; Herwart sent the volumes in batches on loan, naming them with their catalogue numbers — the Tabule regine Elisabeth (Venice 1503), Gemma Frisius's De radio astronomico, Camerarius's Astrologia, the Louvain tables and Peurbach's Theoricae in the first parcel, then Virdung, Apollonius, Bianchini, Pruckner, Reinhold and Peurbach — loans, never Tycho's property, which he undertook to return by a fixed day.111 Norlind, counting from the same letters, reckons some twenty works sent by Herwart and names among them, beyond these, the tables of Alphonsus de Corduba and of King Alfonso, Bersius’s tables, Copernicus’s De lateribus et angulis triangulorum, Gernardus’s Algorithmus demonstratus, a collection of the ancient geographers and Vieta’s Opera mathematica; from Kepler’s letters he adds two more that Tycho had from Herwart, Simon Marius’s Tabulae directionum and Riccius’s De motu octavae sphaerae.112

He sought the papers and books of dead astronomers. In December 1590 he asked Magini for any manuscripts or books of Domenico Maria Novara, Copernicus's teacher;113 in March 1592, having learnt that Hagecius had inherited the library of Rheticus, he asked him for copies, at Tycho's own expense, of whatever astronomical material Rheticus had had from Copernicus or had written himself — Hagecius had already given him, at Regensburg in 1575, the copy of Copernicus's Commentariolus from which the surviving transcripts are held to descend;114,115 Scaliger, asked in 1598 and 1599 for a better text of al-Battani, could not supply one, but sent the stellar observations of Ptolemy and Hipparchus in Greek with his own annotations;116 through Herwart Tycho tried to reach the manuscripts of Cyprian Leovitius in the Fugger library at Augsburg;117 and from Paul Melissus, librarian of the Palatina, he asked in 1600 only about its astronomical manuscripts.118 Walde showed in 1938 that the Vienna transcript of the Commentariolus (Cod. 10530) had belonged to Johannes Eriksen of Hamburg, later Tycho's amanuensis, to whom Longomontanus gave it as a parting gift at Benátky on 18 July 1600; Curtze and Prowe had assumed that it passed to Tycho and reached Vienna with his papers, and Walde doubted it, since Eriksen's other books probably entered Queen Christina's library with the Prague booty.119

Several times he tried to buy a whole library or a large part of one. An attempt on the books of a Dr Ambrose of Augsburg was frustrated in 1575, and Tycho learnt of their dispersal by the heirs of the next owner years too late;120 in 1576, with Joachim Camerarius as intermediary, he tried for the mathematical books of one Brechtalius, knowing that most of the library had already been sold;121 and at Benátky in May 1600, shown by Baron Karl von Wartenberg a catalogue of Dudith's library, he asked Jacob Monaw to learn from the heirs whether he might see the books that had pleased him most and whether they would sell some separately, since they would hardly find a buyer for the whole. Monaw replied that the library ‘now hangs as if between heaven and earth’, neither taken over by the heirs nor sold but pawned, and ‘lies wretchedly nearby in a certain house, no differently … than heaps of grain lie strewn in noble households’.122 Mosley's summary: ‘in this case, as in the others, Tycho's hopes were frustrated’.

The exception, and a partial one, was the library of Paul Wittich, who had spent the summer and autumn of 1580 on Hven with his annotated copies of De revolutionibus.123 Westman counts the surviving annotated copies as five, the fifth, with a single annotation, identified in Gingerich's census.124 On Wittich's departure in October 1580 Tycho gave him Apianus's Astronomicum Caesareum, bought for twenty florins, inscribed ‘to a friend and fellow lover of mathematics’ — the copy is in Chicago.125,126,127 Tycho learnt of Wittich's death from Rothmann in September 1587. In March 1589 he wrote to Hagecius that he had asked two noble visitors, the barons of Herberstein and Liechtenstein, to obtain the library, and Hagecius recruited Monaw in Wrocław as agent.128 Christianson counts three attempts: through Hagecius and the two barons in 1589, through Hagecius and Monaw in 1595, and through Longomontanus and Monaw in 1598, when Wittich's sister showed Longomontanus her brother's books.129 Tycho's instructions to Hagecius are explicit: ‘I especially want his Copernicus and what he wrote on trigonometry, and also other manuscripts if there are any’; the Apianus he did not want back ‘unless it can be bought for a favourable price’.130 To Monaw in January 1600 he named the three copies of Copernicus' De revolutionibus coelestibus that Wittich had ‘annotated with certain marginal notes’ (Exemplaria quae habuit tria Copernici a se marginalibus quibusdam illustrata), ‘what he had written on triangles’ and ‘on the celestial hypotheses, if any are found’. By 16 May 1600 the sister was dead; Tycho now wanted only the books on Longomontanus's list, asked the price of all of them if the guardians of the orphan heir would not sell separately, and failing that a loan of a few weeks.131 Monaw was in Prague in October 1600, and the Copernicus volumes are presumed to have come with him; no letter records a price, a payment or a delivery, and the correspondence ends with his promise of 14 June 1600.132 Wittich had owned four annotated copies. Gingerich and Westman, followed by Mosley, state that Monaw despatched three of them to Prague and that the fourth, which remained in Wrocław, was copied by Valentin von Sebisch about 1604.133 Gingerich's census of 2002 places two in Tycho's hands: the Basel edition of 1566 in Prague (Sign. 14 B 16), inscribed for the Jesuit college in 1642, whose annotations were long taken for Tycho's own until Gingerich and Westman showed them to be Wittich's, and the Nuremberg edition of 1543 in the Vatican (Ott. lat. 1902), which it presumes Tycho obtained through Monaw in October 1600.134,135 Of the other two, the census holds that the Basel copy Sebisch transcribed, now in the University Library at Wrocław, was surely not among those sold to Tycho if the copying was done in Wrocław after 1600, and for the Nuremberg copy at Liège (Rés. MS 69C) it records no owner between Wittich and the university.136,137 The third copy of Gingerich and Westman's count is thus the Liège volume, which Gingerich in the same year supposed to have passed through a Belgian Jesuit working in Prague; the census leaves that passage unrecorded, so that of Wittich's four copies one is documented in Tycho's library, one presumed by the census and one conjectured by Gingerich.138 The National Library's curator, writing in 2026, adds how the Vatican and Prague copies came to be joined: Gingerich, who had found the annotated Nuremberg edition in the Vatican Library in 1973, received Horský's facsimile of the Clementinum copy at a conference in Paris and recognised the same hand and the same sketches in both; the Prague volume, she notes, does not carry the first-person note on fol. 82 whose copy in Edinburgh, with Craig's ‘M. Witt’, gave the hand its name.139

What Monaw is presumed to have brought was a selection. Three other books from Wittich's library are known, none of them in Tycho's hands: a copy of Tycho's own De nova stella of 1573 with Wittich's notes on the lunar-eclipse section, at the Paris Observatory (shelfmark 20540); Sebastian Conradus's Egnatius, sive Quaestura (Basel 1556), signed ‘Paulus Wittich Anno 66 Id Feb’, at Wrocław (452390); and a manuscript of the Timaeus in Calcidius's Latin, now at Leipzig (Cod. Rep. I.4, 84), whose f. 8 records that Thomas Schroeter of Neisse bought it ‘at Breslau from the library of M. Paul Wittich … in the year of Christ 1606’ — six years after the presumed delivery to Prague, Wittich's books were still being sold in his home town.140 Gingerich's own count of the Clementinum Tychoniana, made in June 1978, is the one dated tally by kind of mark: just over fifty items, sixteen in bindings stamped T.B.O., six with the portrait stamp, twelve more identified by the inscribed initials T B (three carry both), two presented to Tycho, one bought for him and at least one — the Scultetus of 14 F 284 — known by his marginalia alone; of twenty-three volumes inscribed for the Prague college in 1642, seventeen add ‘Ex Bibliotheca Tichoniana’, among them Kepler's De stella nova of 1606, which no Jesuit note could have made Tycho's.141 Across those volumes, they found, Tycho ‘was a stinting annotator’: the Prague De revolutionibus and the Triangulorum praxis (14 C 20) hold nearly all the manuscript material, in two hands that are neither each other's nor his.142

Mosley reads the pursuit against Tycho's quarrel with Ursus over the world-system and over prosthaphaeresis, the trigonometrical method whose first identity Wittich had disclosed to Tycho on Hven: Tycho may have wanted Wittich's papers to suppress or to use, and, ever respectful of his mathematics, to learn from them; the pursuit was ‘overdetermined’.143 He notes that Tycho's later account to Herwart (November 1599) — that he had taken the method up some twenty years earlier from a remark of Rheticus, with Werner's book as a further entry-point — sits uneasily with the correspondence, which shows that Tycho possessed no copy of Werner until one was procured for him in November 1588: an ‘apparent discrepancy’ for which, Mosley allows, innocent explanations can be conjectured, though the phrasing is ‘curious enough to suggest an attempt at misdirection’; and that of the two copies of Tycho's letter to Hagecius of 1580 preserved among his papers, only one contains the passage reporting Wittich's acknowledgement of a debt to words Tycho had spoken at Wittenberg.144

2.6 The size of the library

Mortensen, who in 1942 had himself printed the figure of about five thousand volumes,145 by 1955 put the library, from the dimensions of the Museum, at no more than a thousand volumes, about two thousand works, and rejected Walde's private estimate of some five thousand as needing more room than the Museum had; the ‘two chambers full of books’ that the Baron of Mörsberg saw beside the printing office in 1592 he thought might be the stock of Tycho's own works.146 Norlind held it ‘impossible to form any estimate of its size’, and traced the estimate of several thousand to Studnička's inference from the spine number 2781 on Gesner's De omni rerum fossilium genere (Zürich 1565) — valid only if the number came from Tycho or from a catalogue of his library — the reservation Walde had already entered in 1938.147,148 That volume, Prandtl's own, is now in Texas. Prandtl's photograph of the binding, the frontispiece of his catalogue of 1932, already shows the spine without a number, and the photographs of 2026 confirm it: the number Studnička read in 1901 was gone within a generation, between Laube's death in 1923 and Prandtl's purchase of the book from the trade.149,150 No other surviving volume carries a number that can be shown to be Tycho's: the Clementinum lacquered and relabelled the spines of its volumes in 1642 and the Chomutov college painted its own with a letter and a Roman numeral, so no Prague spine can show an earlier mark, and the numbers on the spines of the volumes at Helsingør and Uppsala are later owners'.151 Christianson writes that in March 1597 the familia ‘had completed registration of all his books, over three thousand volumes’, and notes that the only evidence is the one extant volume ‘numbered 2781 during the registration’.152 Nothing joins the number to the registration: the note cites only the diary entry and Norlind, and Studnička's attribution of the hand was an inference from the intact spine, argued against no other example.153 Gingerich, observing that similar numbers are apparently not found in the other surviving volumes, calls the deduction ‘at best speculative’, while allowing that a library of three thousand titles would not be unreasonable beside Rantzau's six thousand, Dee's nearly four thousand, Hoffmann's four thousand and Rožmberk's ten thousand in Prague.154 Westman, writing of the books Tycho bought on the journey of 1575, finds ‘no reason to believe that his library ever approached the size and breadth of Dee's’, and sets Christianson's figure in a note without adopting it.155 The 1597 entry itself gives no number.2 The one visitor who speaks of the books, Fynes Moryson, in Denmark in 1593, writes that Tycho ‘had a very faire Library, full of excellent bookes’, beside the instruments and ‘a like faire still-house’.156 The diary entry of 21 March 1597 stands under one day, three days after Tycho had left for Copenhagen, with only the weather recorded on the 19th and 20th, and Norlind read it as the first registration the library had had.2,1

The room sets the upper bound. Charlier's excavation of 1901 found the south wing's outer foundation and ‘nu intet spår’ of the rotunda's inner wall;157 Albrecht's survey of the same summer, drawn at 1:160, records of the north tower that only the inner ring survives, ‘dessen Radius von ca. 3 m mit dem auf Tycho's Grundriss verzeichneten gut übereinstimmt’, and of the south side nothing but the outer ring and two bricked holes, so that the north tower's ring — 5.95 m over the wall and about 4.8 m inside on the plan — is the one measured analogue for the library room, which the key of 1586 makes its equal.158 Tycho's figures for the towers, 20 feet in 1586 and 22 in 1596, agree with the excavated square, the south wing's outer ring and the north tower's ring as outer measures, at feet of about 29 and 26 cm, and with none of them as inner measures.159 Friis gave the room about 14 feet across, Mortensen 5 m, Jern a little over 5 m from Charlier's drawing and 5.5 m at a foot of 25 cm.160,50,161 A room of 4.8–5.1 m inside has 18–20 m² of floor and 15–16 m of wall, shared with the great globe, the four tables, the smaller globes and instruments and the seven boards between the windows; of the house as a whole Jern writes that ‘anläggningen var förvånansvärt liten’.162 Shelves on the free wall, from knee height to the springing of the vault that Christensen's section of 1921 gives the room, hold about 650–700 volumes of the make of the forty-eight Prague volumes whose library records give their extent — 53 mm thick on average, 2.31 works to a volume; shelves to three metres under a beamed ceiling, and under the window sills, hold about 1,100–1,200; three thousand volumes would need twenty to thirty metres of shelf wall.163 No second room for books is recorded: the keys and plans give every room of the house its use, and the ‘two chambers full of books’ at the printing office are accounted for by the press, whose calendar for 1586 alone was to be printed in two thousand copies.164,146 The figures that follow are the edition's own: a library of about a thousand bound volumes at the departure of 1597, within some seven hundred to twelve hundred, and about two thousand separate works; three thousand works lies at the top of that range, three thousand volumes outside the room.163

2.7 From Hven to Prague, 1597–1601

The last observation on Hven was made on 15 March 1597; on 18 March the pension of 500 dalers, paid since February 1576, was stopped; and on 21 March the books were registered.165 The day of the departure is not settled. Tycho's own account in the observing ledger has the family leave statim a Paschatis Festo die 29 Aprilis; Dreyer, noting that Easter fell on 27 March and that Tycho's German account has him at Copenhagen ‘into the third month’, read the month as a slip and put the departure on 29 March, and Thoren gives the same day without discussion;166,167 Nielsen kept 29 April.168 The diary is silent from 22 March to 10 April and records on 11 April the entry into the ‘new Museum at Copenhagen’ (the removal);169 Christianson, who has the royal commissioners Christian Friis and Axel Brahe on Hven on 9 and 10 April finding the library, the presses and the instruments packed and much already carried across, dates the household's move to the following day.170 Christianson dates the departure from Copenhagen to Saturday 2 June 1597, the household more than a score of people;171 Prandtl has Tycho embark for Rostock in June with his family, students and companions, about twenty persons, and with his goods.172 Norlind gives the stations of the following years — Rostock in 1597, Wandsbek with Rantzau in 1597–98, Wittenberg in 1598–99, Prague in 1599, Benátky in 1599–1600 and Prague again from the summer of 1600 — and remarks that the books and manuscripts probably suffered on the way, that the correspondence hints at books lost or not to be found, and that the library and archive on Hven had hardly been in good order to begin with.173 The Gotha copy of Apianus's Astronomicum Caesareum (Math gr2° 00038/02) carries Tycho's inscription of 14 June 1599, the day he left Wittenberg for Prague.174,175 The entry — Psalm 19:1, a distich and the subscription scripsi with the date — is the only writing of Tycho's in the volume, whose title page bears no owner's name and whose text no annotation.176 Norlind read it as an album autograph rather than an owner's mark, thought it hardly likely that the book had been Tycho's, and held that it may well have belonged to someone else — perhaps even, he suggested, Duke Friedrich Wilhelm of Saxe-Weimar (Norlind: ‘Sachsen-Altenburg’), Administrator of Electoral Saxony, with whom Tycho was then in contact; the Gotha library records the volume as Tycho's gift to the duke.177,178

The library reached Prague late. Norlind traces the shipment: the greater part of the books and manuscripts, with the remaining instruments and household goods, went up the Elbe from Hamburg to Magdeburg, where the cathedral chapter delayed the transit licence; in November 1599 Tycho was still awaiting the library from Magdeburg, in April 1600 the goods were still there, and only after the Emperor intervened did the consignment reach Litoměřice in late summer 1600 and Prague by mid-November; the books and instruments he had at Benátky had been moved to Prague earlier.179 Thoren observes that until the library was pried out of Magdeburg and moved into the Kurtz house, access to another man’s books would have been convenient for Tycho, and points to Baron Hoffmann’s library of four thousand volumes.180 Herwart’s loans from Munich began in the same interval, the first parcel announced from Munich on 3 April 1600.111 The last purchases dated by Tycho’s own notes are the Christmann (14 J 164/adl. 1) and the Reinhold (14 J 176), whose purchase notes of May and June 1601 are quoted above; Magini’s Ephemerides had reached him from Frankfurt by 24 January 1601, when he acknowledged their receipt.181,182 Tycho died at Prague on 24 October 1601 and was buried in the Týn church on 4 November.183

2.8 The estate and the heirs' books, 1601–1771

Kleinschnitzová summarised the history of the estate from Dvorský, Dreyer and Volf. Immediately after Tycho's death Rudolf II wished to acquire the astronomical part of it — the instruments and the manuscript observations — for his collections; the heirs valued the instruments at 100,000 thaler; when the negotiations led by Stephan Georg von Sternberg, president of the Hofkammer, failed, the whole estate was sequestered and the heirs were threatened with prison; they then ceded the instruments and manuscripts by contract for 20,000 thaler. ‘The library itself remained in the possession of the heirs’, while the instruments cum libris observationum, by her account, were moved to Vienna in 1604 on Rudolf's order, ‘for greater safety’.184 Dreyer, by contrast, has the instruments stored in a vault under the Curtius house in Prague after Tengnagel returned there in the summer of 1602 — Kepler complained repeatedly in his writings of being denied access to them — and, on Gassendi's report, destroyed in the rising of 1619; only the great globe survived, found at Neisse in 1632 by Prince Ulrik and sent to Denmark, where it burnt in 1728.185 Of the purchase price the heirs received 4,000 thaler in September 1603 — Kleinschnitzová’s ‘vorerst nur eine kleine Rate’ — and, in Dreyer’s account, no part of the remainder, which he gives as 15,000 thaler, though Mortensen, after Friis, has 4,000 paid in 1602 and a further 1,000 in 1608, one heir paid in wine, and the matter still unsettled in 1671; petitions to Rudolf, Matthias and Ferdinand II followed, a granddaughter was still in Bohemia pursuing the claim in 1652, and the debt is mentioned in official documents as late as 1685 and 1688.186,187,188 In 1642 Tycho's grandson Rudolf Tycho Tengnagel ceded his share of the imperial bond to the Dominicans of the Old Town, and about the same time Tycho's daughter Sophie, a convert, bequeathed hers to the Franciscans of Kadaň; neither house saw money, and both accepted iron for the repair of their churches from Emperor Leopold instead, the Franciscans in 1672, the Dominicans in 1678.189

The observation books took a different road. Kepler received them, originals and copies, under a contract with Tengnagel of 1604 and never returned them; after his death in 1630 his son Ludwig kept them as a pledge against a bond of 12,694 gulden issued to the family by the court in 1633, and sold them to Frederik III of Denmark — in 1655 for 600 rigsdaler according to Mortensen, by the beginning of 1662 at the latest according to Dreyer; they are in the Royal Library, Copenhagen.190,191 A fair copy in nineteen quarto volumes, bound in parchment and, on all but two, stamped with Tycho's portrait and arms, is in Vienna; Friis, who described it in 1868, found that Mosel's history of the Hofbibliothek dates the acquisition of ‘the excellent library of the famous astronomer Tycho Brahe’ to about 1657, but observes that only part of the manuscripts came there and hardly many of the printed books.192,193

The library stayed with the family. Kleinschnitzová: apart from Frans Gansneb Tengnagel, Tycho's son-in-law, who had meant to continue the scientific work but died in 1622, the family he left ‘could have had little interest in the contents of the library’ and, to live through the long lawsuit, ‘were forced to part with all their possessions little by little; in this way parts of the library certainly passed into other hands’.194 In November 1624 Kepler, answering Georg Brahe, still urged the heirs to have ‘einen vleissigen Catalogum’ drawn up of all that remained of the library in books and manuscripts, and asked in particular for the Greek Hipparchus with Scaliger’s hand.195 One book, Gesner's Bibliotheca (Sign. 2 A 8), was the property of Andreas Scherhackl at Budějovice by 1623, the year after Tengnagel's death, and was given in 1654 to the Jesuit college at Krumlov; two others (5 G 92 and 5 J 103) were given in 1654 by Gabriel Crusius to the college at Chomutov; a Chomutov volume unknown to Kleinschnitzová, the incunable Sammelband 44 F 38 — d'Ailly's Imago mundi with the Ratdolt Euclid of 1482, bound in 1576, with Tycho's monogram and glosses — had been entered in that college's catalogue already in 1645, and the National Library's provenance survey counts at least four Tycho volumes with the stamp of 1576 among the 215 volumes with Chomutov inscriptions it had registered by 2021;196,197 one (18 J 318) reached the Jesuit professed house in the Malá Strana.198 Kleinschnitzová noted that the names Scherhackl and Crusius recur in Walde's history of the libraries carried off to Sweden.199 The same Crusius — nobilis ac doctissimus Dominus Gabriel Crusius, whose mark on 5 G 92 reads ‘G. Crusio inservio’ — wrote the same formula in ink on the title of Ramus's Arithmeticae (AG XIV 7) and his name in the same form, in graphite, on the title of the Euclid of 1558 (AV II 2), two volumes now at Strahov, neither of which carries a Jesuit inscription: at least four books from the library passed through the hands of one named collector in the middle of the seventeenth century, and his holding scattered by more than one route — two volumes to the Chomutov Jesuits in 1654, the Euclid, by a route no document records, to Jan Josef Klauser, who died in 1771, before the suppression.200 Who he was is not established; a Gabriel is recorded among the sons of the physician Daniel Kraus z Krausenbergu (1579–1637) of Jirkov in the Chomutov district, who Latinised his name as Crusius and whose signature is probably the ‘Daniel Crusius’ on a Peuerbach of 1573 in the National Library, but no document names the son as a book-owner.201 Two documents frame the Chomutov road without explaining it. From 1623 the heirs' claim on the Chamber was assigned to the tax arrears of four royal towns, Chomutov and Kadaň among them — the instruction to Chomutov to pay from its arrears is dated 26 September 1623 — and the towns, unable to pay, sent small instalments until 1654;202 and the college that received Tycho's volumes had lost its library to the Saxon plunder of 1631, partly bought back the next year, so that the first dated Tycho accession of 1645 entered a library being rebuilt.203 Dvorský's archival report gives the road its traveller: after the White Mountain the Protestant heirs had to leave Bohemia — ‘where they went we do not know’ — and from 1628 the one of them allowed back, Jørgen, went every half year to Rakovník and to Chomutov for the interest on the towns' arrears, until his death below Křivoklát in 1640; two years later his Catholic sister Sophie and the Catholic grandson Rudolf Tycho Tengnagel, captain of the Malá Strana, were giving the family's claims to the Franciscans of Kadaň and the Dominicans of St Giles in the year the books went to the Clementinum;204

The Swedish occupation of the west bank of Prague in July 1648 carried the Rudolfine collections to Sweden.205 Gingerich traces one of the Wittich copies of De revolutionibus, acquired by Tycho in 1600, along that road: it ‘must have gone into Rudolf's collection’, was taken by Königsmarck, passed into Queen Christina's library and with her to Rome, and through Pope Alexander VIII's Ottoboni collection into the Vatican Library, where he found it in 1973 (Ott. lat. 1902); the Liège copy he supposed, in the same paper, to have come ‘perhaps’ through a Belgian Jesuit working in Prague, a passage his census does not record (above).138 Veselá’s account of the booty gives the road its dates and its limits: the Castle stormed on 26 July 1648 (New Style), its books removed before 8 October and shipped by Dömitz and Wismar to Stockholm by the end of May 1649, with no transport list surviving for Prague; the Castle the one Prague collection confiscated systematically, the Old Town Jesuit college and the university libraries untouched — the reason the core of 1642 was never at risk; and the first Tycho items documented on that road not books of the library but two manuscripts and a print from Rudolf II’s collection, entered in the Stockholm catalogue of 1650–51 under Tengnagel’s name and now untraced.206 Two of her cautions bear on every claim made for this road: the Castle catalogue of 1636 is Ferdinand III’s library, not Rudolf’s, so a book ‘through Rudolf’ must be shown in Rudolf’s hands by other means; and the recataloguing of 1650–51 stripped the booty’s marks, so a volume without a Swedish mark is not thereby excluded from it.207

2.9 The Clementinum and the Jesuit houses, 1642–1778

In 1642 a group of volumes passed from the heirs to the Jesuit college in the Clementinum. Šolc: the year appears on the title pages together with the words Bibliotheca Tychoniana, ‘when Jesuit librarians incorporated the donation from Tycho's heritage into the University library of the Clementinum College. Unfortunately, no inventory was preserved from this time and nothing was also found from the following two centuries.’208 The library's entry in Fabian's Handbuch states instead that the Tycho autographs ‘von seinen Erben 1642 gekauft wurden’ — were bought from the heirs; Volf calls the transfer a gift made once the heirs had turned Catholic, and Kleinschnitzová after him (‘Ein Teil kam als Schenkung an das Jesuiten-Kollegium im Klementinum’); none of them cites a document of the transaction.209,210 Mortensen in 1955 and Norlind in 1970 have the heirs, in want, sell parts of the library, some to the Clementinum college and some to the college at Chomutov.211 Šolc's own later account calls the transfer a donation — ‘věnováno’, the harder-to-sell, predominantly astronomical part of the library together with the Kepler tracts of 1606 the heirs had added — and has the Vitellio–Dürer Sammelband reach the Jesuits ‘probably by sale’ in 1636; his German account of 1999 has the heirs left with the library to sell and the Sammelband ‘presumably’ bought in that year: the balance of the Prague sources now reads donation of the remainder with a separate purchase, but no document is cited for either verb.212 Kleinschnitzová counted twenty-three volumes with the inscription Collegij Caesarei Societatis Jesu Pragae A° 1642, in most cases followed by Ex Bibliotheca Tijchoniana and Musaei Mathematici: the books belonged to the college's Mathematical Museum, a separately administered department whose volumes are recognisable by their spines — white-lacquered, with red-brown double lines along the bands and the title in black, where the rest of the Clementinum carried red shelfmarks on white.213 The Museum was there to receive them: a Sammelband of Ryff's Quaestiones geometricae (Frankfurt 1600) in the National Library, 14 E 3, is inscribed ‘Collegii Caesarei Soctis JESU Pragae Musaei Mathem: A.1638’, four years before the Tycho books were catalogued, and Vydra names Jakub Kresa, last in Prague in 1702–03, first among those who enriched it, so that the formal foundation of 1722 under the rector Retz, with Caspar Pflieger as first prefect, crowned nearly a century of growth.214 The National Library's catalogue of 2024 adds that the Museum was open to the public from 1722 to 1785, that its library catalogue does not survive, and that in the library's last decades, under its keeper Josef Stepling (1716–1778), no ex-libris was written into its books, so that the spine treatment — red lines and an abbreviated author and title — is the mark of that stratum.215 Seydl's history of the Museum gives the frame: the Jesuits kept it until the suppression of 1773 and the observatory's astronomers from 1773 to 1785; in 1781 its portrait prints had already been handed to the library (‘Iconismi bibliothecae traditi’) and Strnad's list of its contents still ended with ‘some books’ — a Koran, a vizier's diary, Verbiest's Chinese ephemerides and Chinese tracts in the third room, a few named volumes in the deposit already handed to the library, and no other astronomy among them; in 1785 the Museum was dissolved as a whole because the University Library needed its rooms for the books of the suppressed monasteries, its instruments going to the observatory and its mechanics to Herget's engineering institute.216

Kleinschnitzová held it certain that the number given to the Clementinum had originally been larger. Not a single work of Hagecius stands among the Prague Tychoniana, although Tycho must have owned many; of the thirty-two books Norlind had listed from the correspondence in 1926, almost all are in the Clementinum, but only seven can be shown by their marks and bindings to be Tycho's copies; and none of Tycho's own works is among the remains.217 Stanislaus Wydra, writing in 1778, recalled that ‘not a few books from Tycho's library’ had come to the Clementinum, among them the rarest, the Astronomiae instauratae mechanica, which he had often had in his hands while the Society lasted; in 1933 Kleinschnitzová could no longer find it in the Clementinum.218 After 1773 the Museum was removed from its place, divided, and finally kept in Stepling's flat, returning to the Clementinum only after his death in 1778; Tycho's Sammelbände were broken up and recombined with books of other provenance (Kleinschnitzová names 5 G 42, 14 J 164, 19 G 20 and the manuscript XII A 12), a practice continued by the last Austrian director, Kukula, to whose ‘relentlessly executed system’ the modern binding of the Scultetus (14 F 284) bears witness; and Tycho had not marked every book, as 14 H 188 and 14 J 192 show, so that further Tychoniana may have ‘simply gone under in the sea of books of the Clementinum’.219

The suppression of the Society in 1773 and the secularisation of the monasteries brought the scattered parts together again in the Clementinum: nineteen volumes came from the college at Chomutov, the library accepting from the dissolved houses only what it did not already hold; with a few strays from elsewhere the University Library held, in 1933, 97 works from Tycho's library in 47 volumes.220 Six of the volumes carry the gilt portrait and arms, eight are marked only by a handwritten T. B., and the rest for the most part carry the brown calf bindings with T. B. O. and a year.221 Gingerich's count of 2002 classifies the Clementinum volumes differently: sixteen with the stamped initials, six with the portrait, twelve with inscribed initials, two identified by an author's presentation; most marginalia in the volumes are by earlier readers, since Tycho bought used books, and clear examples of his own hand are few — in Ziegler's commentary on Pliny (7 B 27) and in Scultetus on the comet of 1577.222 Kleinschnitzová excluded two volumes formerly counted: the manuscript Sammelband XII A 12, whose Tychonianum had been extracted and bound alone, and Kepler's De stella nova of 1606 (14 J 199), which carries Ex Bibliotheca Tijchoniana but was printed five years after Tycho's death. Kukula, who published the volume in 1906–07 with its second tract, Kepler's De Jesu Christi vero anno natalitio (Frankfurt 1606), took the two as proof that the family and Kepler kept the library together after Tycho's death and his widow's in 1604 and ‘in all probability’ enlarged it; Norlind after him read the volume as an accession probably made by the heirs.223,30,31 The marked Tychoniana that were never his are listed separately.

What the remnant is made of, the National Library's own accounts state, and Šolc himself says the division is no guide to the original library. Every book is Latin but for two Greek texts; Danish and German books printed at Uraniborg he supposes once stood on the shelves and are gone. By place of printing: Basel 23, Nuremberg 20, Paris 15, Wittenberg 6, Venice and Heidelberg 5 each, Frankfurt am Main 4, Leipzig, Strasbourg and Tübingen 3 each, Antwerp, Bologna, Geneva, Prague, Rome and Vienna 2 each, and one each from Freiburg im Breisgau, Görlitz, Oppenheim, Rostock, Schleswig, Urbino, Verona and Zürich ‘und anderen Orten’.224 By subject: astronomy about 53 titles, philosophy and dialectic 16, mathematics 14, astrology 10, and fewer in history, classical literature, physics including optics, geography, meteorology, grammar, metallurgy, medicine, botany and biography (his Czech text of 2006 gives these as 6, 6, 4, 3, 3, 2, 2, 1, 1 and 2). The fragment, in his reading, holds the principal contemporary astronomical works almost complete, with astrological and geographical works, Rantzau's historical writings, Joseph Scaliger's astronomical and Julius Caesar Scaliger's linguistic works; it has no prose or poetry, and none of the astronomical or astrological writing of the low level then common on the market, the comet dissertations of Basel and Heidelberg 1580 (14 H 192) being the one exception; the Tetrabiblos and the works of Nostradamus, which Tycho knew and must have owned, are absent — because, he suggests, such books sold well from the estate — and he supposes that the library once held all of Tycho's own works and every Hven imprint, none of which survives in it.225 The volumes themselves show how they were used: Šolc points to the trimmed edges of the Clementinum Sammelbände — the astronomical tracts, the tables above all, are thumbed by frequent use, while the edges of a classical text in the same convolute are nearly untouched and some of its leaves are still joined from the trimming of the volume — and takes it as evidence that classical, historical and philosophical literature was not lacking in Tycho's library but is thinly represented among the Clementinum remains (Šolc 2006, printed p. 191).226

2.10 After 1773: the Clementinum's losses and the volumes at Strahov

The National Library describes the mechanism of the losses. From 1773 the books of the dissolved houses were brought to the Clementinum and sorted by Karel Rafael Ungar (1744–1807), who discarded duplicates; the library of the Mathematical Museum arrived among the last, ‘so many of its books were discarded. Part of the surplus astronomical volumes were bought for himself by the director of the Clementinum observatory, Antonín Strnad, and from his estate they came to the Strahov library.’227 Strahov bought a substantial part of Strnad's library after his death in 1799.228 Munzar and Pařez, writing in 2002, supposed that the Strahov purchases from the Clementinum stock were made ‘probably not before the abolition of the Jesuit Order in 1773’, and counted five books from Tycho's library at Strahov.229 Felix Mainx, describing the library for bibliophiles in 1940, already gave its original editions of Copernicus, Tycho Brahe and Kepler as coming ‘from the possession of the Prague astronomer A. Strnad’, naming no title, year or shelfmark, and counted among its treasures two Stammbücher with entries of Melanchthon and of Brahe — a witness to the Strnad stratum written three years before he reported the eight supralibros volumes, and silent on them.230

Two volumes now at Strahov carry the Jesuit inscription of 1642 and therefore left the Clementinum. Rheticus and Otho's Opus palatinum of 1596 (Σ V 5/a) is bound in parchment with the full-size portrait and arms plaques; Norlind conjectured it to be the ‘Opus illud Palatinum’ that Tycho's letter to Landgrave Moritz of 10 November 1600 names as a gift sent him in return — a passage struck out in the draft — and the volume was carried in his list on that reading until the Strahov copy was identified.231,232 The Regiomontanus (DR VII 19) consists of Ratdolt's Augsburg almanac of 1492 preceded by the prefatory gatherings of his Venice Ephemerides of 1481; Boldan has shown that the National Library's 1481 Ephemerides (Kleinschnitzová 14) lacks the first gatherings a4 and a10, of which the Strahov volume preserves a4 and two leaves of a10, so that the two are parts of one book, both inscribed in 1642.233 On the verso of the leaf for December 1495 the volume carries a Latin note of thirty-four lines on a severe winter and the epidemic that followed, which Straka published in 1913 and attributed to Tycho's hand on a comparison he did not set out; Munzar and Pařez, comparing the letterforms with Tycho's entry in Plan's album and his dedication in the Strahov Mechanica, found the hand ‘with a high probability’ Tycho's and dated the winter to 1495/96; the attribution rests on these two comparisons.234,235 Straka gave the route Clementinum – Strnad – Strahov for the volume; the library's own record places it in the Clementinum's Mathematical Museum before it came to Strahov but finds it in neither published list of Strnad's library, which are the test of the second step.236

A third volume reached Strahov without passing through the Clementinum: the Euclid of 1558 with the name-stamp of 1567 carries no Jesuit inscription; its title bears the mark of Gabriel Crusius, and its next recorded owner is Jan Josef Klauser (Clauser), the Prague printer and gubernial archivist, who died in 1771 and whose library the monastery bought in 1775.237 Klauser's books are known by his printed ex-libris and Strnad's by his handwritten name or his name-stamp; the titles of the Euclid and the Ramus carry the library's round stamps, the Euclid's also its ink inscription.238 Two more carry Tycho's plaques: Ramus's Arithmeticae libri duo of 1580 (AG XIV 7), in gilt-tooled brown leather with the full-size dies, Crusius's formula on its title and no Jesuit inscription, and a convolute of Olympia Morata, Fungerus and Gwalther (Σ VI 3), in leather with the small dies, 58 × 43 mm, whose route is undocumented; Pařez published all four supralibros volumes in 2009 and credited the discovery of the Euclid to Hedvika Kuchařová.239 Two presentation copies at Strahov were never part of the library: the Mechanica in green silk with a bound-in watercolour, Tycho's gift to Jan Zbyněk Zajíc z Házmburka (AG XI 56), and a Progymnasmata with a full-length watercolour of which Šafařík distributed photolithographs in 1881 and which Studnička reproduced in 1901 from a Progymnasmata dated 1603 at Strahov; not traced since.240,241 The library catalogues a Progymnasmata of 1603 (AG XIV 17) with the provenance of the Cistercians of Žďár and no note of Tycho or Házmburk; whether it is the volume Šafařík saw is not established.242

2.11 Volumes outside Prague

Books left the library in Tycho's lifetime as gifts. The Vedel volume of 1563 at Uppsala, first reported by Walde in 1920, is one on Walde's reading of it as Tycho's book given to Vedel; Norlind read the gift the other way, as Vedel's book given to Tycho, who had it bound with two others, and Walde later allowed an exchange of books between the companions on their journey;243,20,244 the other is a Sammelband of Calvin's Epistolae et responsa (Hanau 1597) and Theocritus (Heidelberg 1596), bound in parchment with the small portrait and arms plaques and inscribed Johanni Lindenouio consobrino suo Tycho Brahe ddt, which the Royal Library acquired from America in the 1950s (the Lindenov volume); the Calvin edition postdates the registration of March 1597, so the gift to his cousin Hans Lindenov was made after Tycho left Hven — probably before 1599, Mortensen supposed, unless the book was sent to Lindenov later.245 Two Danish copies known from earlier record are lost: Lipsius's De constantia, with Tycho's critical remarks of 1592 on the first leaf, recorded by Jens Kragh Høst in 1822 as in the University Library at Copenhagen, and Niels Krag's De republica Lacedaemoniorum (1593) with Tycho's distichs, expected from Rostgaard's bequest of 1745 and not found.246

Other volumes surfaced in the trade or in private hands. Agrippa's Opera, tomus I (Lyon, c. 1550), a parchment binding with the plaques, was in Harald Mortensen's own books when Prandtl listed it in 1932 and in the Tycho Brahe case at Frederiksborg when Mortensen wrote in 1955; the museum keeps it in its library as inventory B3950a-b.247,248,249 The last tome of Zwinger's Theatrum humanae vitae (Basel 1587), titled volumen XVIII and holding volumina XVIII–XXIX with the index, a Renaissance binding carrying his plaques, was with the Munich bookseller Ludwig Rosenthal in 1906, when its two plaques were reproduced in the Zeitschrift für Bücherfreunde, whose writer took the binding for Basel work made for Tycho; Bargum, replying from Copenhagen, argued that it was Danish work made under Tycho's direction and that Tycho may have taken from its title the name of his projected Theatrum astronomicum; Mortensen judged it a binding Tycho had acquired bound and then stamped with the plaques;250,251 it passed from Per Hierta's library at Främmestad to the Röhsska museum in Göteborg.252 Walde reported in 1938 from the library of the Staatliche Akademie at Braunsberg a copy of Scaliger's De emendatione temporum in the Leiden edition of 1598, bound in white parchment with the portrait and arms blind-stamped, its many marginalia in several hands, of which the samples photographed for Hans Ræder were judged not to be Tycho's, and a note of M. Braunhardt at Gotha, 1761, ‘ex bibliotheca Oppeliana’; its later fate is not recorded in anything held here (the entry).253,254 Isak Collijn reported to Kleinschnitzová a Paulus Alexandrinus of 1588 in Tychonic binding at the Landesbibliothek in Dresden and a De nova stella in the Waldstein library at Mnichovo Hradiště; the Dresden volume was lost in the war, according to the library's report to Norlind, and the Waldstein copy is one of Tycho's own works and falls to its census.255,256 The Alphonsine Tables with Lave Urne's dedication of 1590, formerly in the Prague Jesuit college, were reported to Mortensen in 1943 from the library of the Technische Hochschule in Vienna.97 Felix Mainx told Mortensen in 1938 that he had acquired Tycho's copy of Cornelius Gemma, and in 1943 that he had found in a Prague monastery library eight further books with Tycho's supralibros and many marginalia in his hand, which he meant to describe; no description appeared.257 The Gemma was offered by Martayan Lan in New York in 2001 and is in private hands in America; a copy of Apianus's Astronomicum Caesareum, which Gingerich took for the one Tycho kept when he gave Wittich his, bound for Tycho in 1583 with Schöner's equatorium and stamped T.B.O. 1583, came from a monastery library in the Low Countries by way of a Belgian owner to an American collector about 2001 (the volume of 1583).258 Friis reported in 1868–69 that several of Tycho's books, some with his marginalia, were said to be in the Nostitz library in Prague; Mortensen could not confirm it.259,260 Prandtl's own volume, the Gesner of 1565, bought at Leipzig by G. C. Laube of Prague, went with Prandtl's books to the University of Texas in the 1950s.261,262 Bianchini's Tabulae of 1526, inscribed Ex Bibliotheca Tichoniana and, for the Jesuit college at Prague, Collegii Caesarei Societatis Iesu Pragae anno 1642, are at the monastery of Teplá, catalogued by the National Library.263

2.12 The identification of the surviving volumes, 1868–2025

Friis, who travelled to Prague and Vienna in 1868 to collect material on Tycho, wrote that of the printed books ‘only very few are now known which are known with certainty to come from Tycho Brahe's library’; in Prague he named the Rheticus of 1551 with the 1642 inscription and the trigonometrical Praxis appended to it (14 C 20), the Copernicus 14 B 16, whose marginalia Hanslik’s description of the library (1851) had given to Tycho and which he already declined to give him,264 and the Ptolemy of 1551 (5 B 70), whose purchase note he read as Tycho's own.265,266 Gigas described the bindings of the Uraniborg bindery in 1900.56 Studnička described the Prague Tychoniana for the tercentenary of 1901: the Ptolemy, the Copernicus (whose annotations he took for Tycho's), the Ramus, the Maestlin–Clavius volume and the Scaliger, with the Strahov Mechanica and four manuscripts he held to be autograph: Tycho’s entries in his son’s album and in Siebold Plan’s, the Triangulorum praxis and the Tabulae sinuum.267 A second pamphlet of the same year reported what the custos Josef Truhlář had since brought to his notice in the University Library: the astrological Sammelband with the Sacrobosco manuscript once bound before it (14 F 16, VII E 29), the Reinhold of 1551, the Archimedes volume (5 C 31), a geometrical manuscript of six leaves (XII A 28) and further volumes by title.268 He distinguished three periods of binding — white leather richly decorated, plain brown leather with the initials T. B. O., and the bindings with the distichs — and noted that Tycho had the titles of the works in a Sammelband stamped into its gilt edges.269 Prusík listed in 1901 the Clementinum Tychoniana found in the recataloguing — four manuscripts and sixteen printed works, in four groups by their bindings;270 Kukula in 1906–07 counted five manuscripts and fifty printed books in twenty-eight volumes, seven of the volumes found since 1902;271,272 Straka published the Strahov Regiomontanus and its note in 1913.234 Dreyer's edition of the Opera omnia (1913–29) printed the correspondence in which the acquisitions are documented, and the meteorological diary with the entry of 21 March 1597.273 Norlind listed in 1926 thirty-two numbered works, one of them unprinted, with the loans listed separately, attested by statements of Tycho and his correspondents, chiefly in the letters of the Hven years.274,275

Mortensen described the two ownership marks in 1931.276 Prandtl published in 1932 the first catalogue: Verzeichnis A, fifty-eight printed works and one manuscript in twenty-nine volumes, fifteen of them Sammelbände, by subject and author; Verzeichnis B, the volumes in the order of their acquisition, with their owners in 1932 — his own among them; and the periodisation of the bindings that the concordance still uses.277 Kleinschnitzová's census of 1933 raised the Clementinum count to 97 works in 47 volumes, twenty-one of them unknown to the earlier literature, arranged by provenance — the Mathematical Museum, the volumes from Chomutov, and a group of uncertain origin — and gave the dispersal its documentary frame.278 Walde published the Braunsberg Scaliger in 1938,253 and Felix Mainx told Mortensen in the same year that he had acquired Tycho’s Gemma;257 Mortensen counted 110 works in 57 volumes in 1942;279 Mainx reported in 1943 that he had found eight more books with Tycho’s supralibros and marginalia in a Prague monastery library, finds he meant to publish;257 and Mortensen, gathering the volumes outside Prague in 1955, counted 67 volumes with 121 works.280 Norlind's reconstruction of 1970, Bilaga I of his biography, lists some 216 titles from the surviving volumes and from the books named in Tycho's writings and letters, with the dated bindings as the key to when a book entered the library.281 Gingerich and Westman established in 1988 that the annotations in the Prague and Vatican copies of Copernicus are Wittich's;282 Šolc recounted the Clementinum holding at about 110 titles in 50 volumes (1999, 2002) and added shelfmarks and constituents to Kleinschnitzová's list;283 Gingerich in 2002 put the located books at about 120 and added the dispersed volumes in Texas, Chicago, the Vatican and private hands;284 Šíma and Valeška concluded the same year that none of the three Prague manuscripts Studnička had published as autograph is in Tycho’s hand: the Praxis and the Compendium are by two different professional writers, and a professional writer is ‘highly probable’ for the Tabulae sinuum.285 Kroupa compared the three Bohemian copies of the Mechanica in 2001; Pařez published the Strahov supralibros in 2009; the National Library's catalogue of 2024 set out the marks and the eighteenth-century routes out of the Clementinum; and Boldan's census of Ratdolt's astronomica (2025) identified the two 1481 gatherings bound before the Strahov Regiomontanus as those missing from the National Library's Ephemerides of 1481 (40 F 39).286 In 2023 Boldan and Kazanskii, reading defaced provenance notes in the National Library's incunables with a video spectral comparator, published the Sammelband 44 F 38, with Tycho's monogram and glosses and a Chomutov note of 1645, which Kleinschnitzová’s census had not recorded.196

Of the 251 entries, 108 are works attested as Tycho's of which no copy is located; the desiderata name those with a trace to follow.
