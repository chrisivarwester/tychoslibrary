How the books came to Tycho, and by what roads they left the library — a few in his own lifetime, the rest after 1601. The acquisition channels are reconstructed from the correspondence and from the purchase-notes and dedications on the surviving copies. The roads of dispersal are the routes by which the volumes reached their present holders, each with the marks it leaves on a book; the chain of custody of every located volume is set out station by station, each step with the record that documents it, and where the record is silent the gap is stated as a gap. The figure reads the chains as one descent, from the library to the 14 institutions and the private hands that hold its volumes today.

Every located volume is assigned to one road; the counts sum to the 72 volumes identified as Tycho’s. Road V has no located volume by definition, and the 108 titles without a located copy (§13) are not its measure: a title stands there because no copy has been identified, not because one is known to be lost.

The roads in detail

0 — Left the library in Tycho’s lifetime: gifts and exchanges 4 volumes

Books that left the library in Tycho’s lifetime: the Apianus he gave Paul Wittich at Hven on 29 October 1580, the Apianus at Gotha in which he wrote a dated motto for Duke Friedrich Wilhelm of Saxe-Weimar on leaving Wittenberg on 14 June 1599 — his own book given at parting, as the Gotha library reads the entry, or the duke’s book in which he wrote, as Norlind thought more likely —, the Calvin–Theocritus Sammelband dedicated to his cousin Hans Lindenov in exile, and — if Walde and Mortensen are right against Norlind — the Sammelband of 1563 that carries Vedel’s name. These volumes left the library before it was dispersed and reached their holders by roads of their own.

I — The Jesuit road: the Clementinum (1642), the colleges at Chomutov and Krumlov and the professed house, reunited in the Clementinum after 1773 51 volumes

Tycho's son-in-law Frans Gansneb Tengnagel — a Catholic convert who ran the posthumous editions — had intended to continue Tycho’s work and died in 1622 (Kleinschnitzová 1933, p. 76); the heirs, pressed by the lawsuit over the unpaid purchase price, parted with their possessions piecemeal, the earliest dated step out of the library being of 1623 (p. 76); a part of the library came as a gift to the Jesuit Collegium Caesareum at the Clementinum in Prague (Kleinschnitzová 1933, p. 76, after Volf). The volumes so given — twenty-three survive — carry on the title ‘Collegij Caesarei Societatis Jesu Pragae A° 1642’, in most cases with ‘Ex Bibliotheca Tijchoniana’ and ‘Musaei Mathematici’, and were shelved as the separately kept Musaeum Mathematicum, whose books are known by the red-brown double lines on their white spines (p. 78). A larger group came to the Jesuit college at Chomutov (Komotau), apparently in the same way (p. 76); a single volume each reached the college at Krumlov — by exchange in 1654 from Andreas Scherhackl of Budějovice, who had owned it since 1623 — and the professed house on Prague’s Malá Strana (pp. 76 and 78); the suppression of the Society in 1773 and the secularisation of the monasteries brought these fragments together again in the Clementinum (p. 78) — nineteen volumes of Chomutov provenance by 1933 (p. 81). Šolc's accounts of 1999 (§ 2.533, printed p. 150) and 2006 (printed p. 192) give the nineteen the Chomutov inscription ‘Collegij Societis JESU Commotouij Catalogo inscriptus A.’ with a year between 1671 and 1675 — the 1645 entry of 44 F 38 and Crusius's gifts of 1654 lie outside that range — and add that four further Tychonic volumes reached the Clementinum itself as gifts (‘Stiftungen’) of private persons whose names stand on their title pages; he names neither the donors nor the volumes. The store's Crusius volumes (5 G 92, 5 J 103) were gifts to the Chomutov college, not to the Clementinum, so they are not Šolc's four; which four he means is open (see lead-crusius-layer). The National Library’s provenance survey of its Jesuit stocks (Císařová Smítková, Paličková & Vackářová 2021, p. 30) registers 215 volumes with Chomutov inscriptions, seven of them given to the college before 1600, and among them ‘minimálně čtyři knihy z pozůstalosti Tychona Brahe’, all with the supralibros ‘TBO 1576’ on the front board and the Chomutov inscription on the title; its authors connect the college’s unusual holding of mathematics and astronomy with P. Jan Jüngling (1705–1775), keeper of the Clementinum’s mathematical library in 1749 and prefect of the Chomutov library in 1765–1766, and suppose these books to have been duplicates of the Clementinum college’s library. The Museum itself was removed after 1773 to the lodging of its last keeper, Joseph Stepling, and returned only after his death in 1778, a custody in which losses occurred, at Karel Rafael Ungar's cataloguing of the 1780s the Tycho volumes were then distributed among the University Library's subject departments, predominantly department 14 (Šolc 2006, printed p. 192) — he does not say what the first element of a present shelfmark denotes —, and the librarians of the Clementinum later broke up some of Tycho's Sammelbände and bound his books with others' (p. 80), which is why some of his volumes now hold works that were never his. In the chains of custody the Mathematical Museum is named as a station only where the volume itself carries the words (‘Musaei Mathematici’, ‘Bibliothecae Mathematicae’ or the Museum's spine marking); the college alone is named otherwise. Gingerich's 2002 survey of the Clementinum volumes counts the marks as follows: sixteen carry the initials 'T.B.O.' tooled on the binding, six the gilt supralibros portrait, twelve the initials written in by hand, and two are author presentations (Bruno and Scaliger). Clear marginalia in Tycho's hand appear in only a few — among them the Ziegler In C. Plinii of 1531, in its Martianus Capella section, and Bartholomaeus Scultetus's Cometae of 1577. Šolc's own later wording (2006, p. 192) is ‘věnováno’ — donated — for the harder-to-sell, mostly astronomical remainder, with the Vitellio–Dürer volume reaching the Jesuits ‘probably by sale’ in 1636; the Handbuch's ‘gekauft’ (Faltysová 1999, § 1.13, after Tobolka) is the other reading, and neither cites a document. Two documents frame the Chomutov road without explaining it: in 1623 the heirs' claim on the Chamber was assigned to the tax arrears of four royal towns, Chomutov and Kadaň among them — the instructions to the towns dated Chomutov 26 September 1623 and Kadaň 3 July 1626 (Volf 1932, part 2, p. 23), and the college that received Tycho's volumes had lost its library to the Saxon plunder of 1631, partly bought back the next year (Veselá 2025, pp. 27–29), so that the first dated accession of 1645 entered a library being rebuilt. Whether either tie carried books is undocumented. The duplicates road under Jüngling cannot account for the dated Chomutov entries: he was prefect of the Chomutov library in 1765–66, a century after the inscriptions of 1645–1675, so for the inscribed volumes the choice is between the heirs, the college's own purchases and local private donors such as Crusius (the research round of 27 October 2026).

II — The heirs’ dispersal into private hands (after 1601) 4 volumes

During the lawsuit over the purchase price the heirs parted with their possessions piecemeal, and parts of the library with them: ‘so manches Werk wanderte auch in Privatbesitz’ (Kleinschnitzová 1933, p. 76). The volumes placed here are those whose first recorded owner after Tycho is a private collector of the seventeenth century; what else left by this road is untraced. Since 20 October 2026 the road also carries the Strahov Euclid of 1558 (AV II 2), formerly on a road of its own (VII): it passed from the heirs to Gabriel Crusius — his graphite ‘G. Crusio ‹K›’ on the title, the formula his gift volumes at Chomutov carry in ink — and from him by private hands to Jan Josef Klauser (1705–1771), whose library Strahov bought in 1775; it carries no Jesuit inscription. The Ramus AG XIV 7 began the same way, Crusius giving it to the Chomutov college on 5 August 1654. Kleinschnitzová's 2 A 8 (e-kl-43), inscribed by Andreas Scherhackl of Budějovice in 1623 and given by him to the Krumlov Jesuits by exchange in 1654, belongs here too, not on the Jesuit road: its first owner after the heirs is a private man.

III — The Swedish booty of 1648 (Königsmarck’s capture of the west bank of Prague) 1 volume

The 1648 Swedish capture of Prague carried off Rudolfine/Bohemian holdings, including Tycho material, to Sweden. The first Tycho items documented on this road are not books of the library: two manuscripts — a parchment copy of the star catalogue and a Solis et Lunae motus diarii of 1598–99 — and a printed book in a presentation binding, from Rudolf II’s collection at the Castle, shipped with the Castle’s books in 1648–49 and entered in the Stockholm royal library’s catalogue of 1650–51 under Frans Tengnagel’s name, all three untraced (Veselá 2025, p. 142). The Castle was the one Prague collection the Swedes confiscated systematically; the Old Town Jesuit college and the university libraries were untouched, which is why the core of 1642 was never at risk (p. 256). The recataloguing at Stockholm in 1650–51 stripped the booty’s spine marks, and Vossius removed the royal library’s signatures from the books he took in 1654: a volume without a Swedish mark is not thereby excluded from this road (pp. 208–214, 228). The database of the Swedish book plunder (Knihovna AV ČR, searched in full on 19 October 2026: 4,367 records) holds no Tycho imprint from its Prague-Castle group of 79 records (47 under Rudolf II, 20 under Ferdinand III) and no book of Tycho's own; its three Tycho imprints came by other roads — Dudith's De mundi with the Dietrichstein library of Mikulov (Stockholm KB), Hermann Bulder's De mundi and Tycho's dedication copy of the Mechanica to Petr Vok with the Rožmberk library (Stockholm University Library; Copenhagen). Tengnagel's name returns nothing.

IV — Rudolfine-imperial absorption 0 volumes

Rudolf II had the instruments and the manuscript observations ceded to him by contract (Kleinschnitzová 1933, p. 75), and books may have passed into the court’s orbit with them; none is documented. No located volume is placed on this road: the one candidate, the Vienna manuscript of the Commentariolus, enters the record by an undocumented step and stands on the road of unknown route. Veselá reads the three Stockholm items — two manuscripts and a print — as Tycho’s personal gift to Rudolf II on his arrival in Prague, an inference she marks as such (2025, p. 142). The one catalogue of the Castle library, of 1636 (Riegger 1795, pp. 214–230), is Ferdinand III’s library and not Rudolf’s, whose books had largely gone to Vienna after 1612, and a Habsburg supralibros is not proof of Rudolf’s ownership: any claim that a book of Tycho’s passed through Rudolf’s hands has to pass that test (pp. 140, 150).

V — Attrition / loss

War, fire and untraced dispersal account for the gap between the library as it stood at his death and the remnant located today — no single catastrophe, but a long attrition that left no mark to search by. No located volume belongs here by definition, and the titles without a located copy are not its measure: a title is unlocated because no copy has been identified, not because one is known to be lost; the size of the loss is bounded only by the size of the library, which is itself disputed.

VI — Out of the Jesuit stock after 1773 without staying in the Clementinum: Strahov (Strnad), Teplá, Kórnik 4 volumes

Two volumes carrying the 1642 inscription are now at Strahov, not in the Clementinum: Rheticus–Otho's Opus palatinum (Σ V 5/a) and the Regiomontanus Ephemerides (DR VII 19). The documented route out of the Clementinum is the one Boldan & Vackářová describe for the Mathematical Museum's library: after the suppression of 1773 Karel Rafael Ungar screened the incoming Jesuit and monastic books for duplicates, the observatory director Antonín Strnad bought part of the surplus astronomical volumes for himself, and from his estate they passed to Strahov, which bought the substantial part of his library after his death in 1799 (Munzar 2001). Straka gave exactly this chain for the Regiomontanus in 1913 — Clementinum → Strnad → Strahov — though Strahov's present cataloguer notes that the volume appears in neither published catalogue of Strnad's library (Seydl 1939; Kouklová 1989). The spine is the test: the Mathematical Museum's books carry no ex-libris, only red lines and an abbreviated title on the spine, and Strnad's books his handwritten name or name-stamp. Seydl identified the Strahov volumes from Strnad's library by matching his own book-list, in two hands, against the Strahov volume catalogue (Hyklová 2017, p. 49) — the method behind the cataloguer's finding that the Regiomontanus is in neither list. The library's own record of DR VII 19 nevertheless places the volume in the Clementinum's Mathematical Museum before it came to Strahov, and Munzar and Pařez (2002, p. 369) supposed the Strahov purchases from the Clementinum stock were made ‘probably not before’ 1773. Two volumes show the same leakage from other Jesuit stocks. The Bianchini of Teplá, inscribed at the Clementinum in 1642, is now in the Premonstratensians' library there by an undocumented step. Heidenstein's De bello Moscovitico (Basel 1588), in Tycho's portrait-and-arms binding and catalogued at Chomutov in 1671, never reached the Clementinum at all: it is at the Biblioteka Kórnicka (Cim.Qu.2812), its route there — the volume carries older shelfmarks and stamps not yet identified — still to be established. Kleinschnitzová (1933, p. 81) supposed that the Chomutov stock reached the Clementinum only in part; the Kórnik volume is the first located proof of it. The Bianchini's Teplá station closed in 2006–07, when the National Library bought the Teplá monastery's manuscripts, incunabula and paleotypes and the volume returned to the Clementinum (NK ČR STT 000005829, accession 07/ORST 394); the road keeps Teplá's name as the house it passed through.

VIII — With the observation books: Kepler, Ludwig Kepler, Frederik III (after 1604) 1 volume

The manuscript observations went a road of their own: Kepler received them under a contract with Tengnagel of 1604 and never returned them; after his death in 1630 his son Ludwig held them as a pledge against the court’s bond of 1633 and sold them to Frederik III of Denmark — in 1655 on Mortensen’s account (1955, pp. 31–32), ‘at the latest in the beginning of 1662’ on Dreyer’s (1890, Appendix, pp. 370, 373). A leaf of another man’s observations sewn into Tycho’s own codex travelled with them.

  6. Oscar Ekman, engineer, and Lia Ekman, Copenhagen until 1957
    The museum's report of its acquisitions of 1956–57 (Klem, Årbog 1957, p. 8; Thykier 2003 dates the gift to January 1957 and names Oskar Ekman alone): ‘en gave fra fru Lia Ekman og afdøde ægtefælle, civilingeniør Oscar Ekman’ [a gift from Mrs Lia Ekman and her late husband, the engineer Oscar Ekman]. Oscar Ekman (b. Copenhagen 13 October 1885, owner of Nordisk Elektricitets Selskab from 1913, d. 16 September 1956) had been a life member of the museum's friends' society since 1944–45; Lia Ekman (née Pouplier, b. Hamburg 1894, d. 1965) remained one to her death. Their source for the volume is not recorded; at the sale of their library (Bruun Rasmussen 186, November 1965) it was shown as a withdrawn show-piece, as Erik Dal reported in Bogvennen 1966. The museum's accession note on the rear pastedown, ‘Gave jan. 1957 fra civiling. Oskar Ekman’ [gift, January 1957, from the engineer Oskar Ekman] (read 20 October 2026), enters the gift in January 1957 under Oscar's name, four months after his death: a gift in the dead man's name, made by his widow — which is what Klem's wording, Thykier's and Dal's ‘testamentarisk bortgivet’ [given away by bequest] each describe. The sale catalogue itself (Bruun Rasmussen 186, p. 111, lot 497, read 29 October 2026) lists the volume under ‘En Bog fra Tycho Brahe's Bibliotek’ for completeness only — ‘This copy will not be sold as it was given to the “Handels- og Søfartsmuseet” at Kronborg according to the last will of ingeniør Ekman’; the Danish text ‘foræret … af ingeniør Ekman og kan beses der i en af udstillingsmontrerne’ — so the gift was a bequest under Oscar's will, executed by his widow in January 1957, and the volume stood in a showcase at Kronborg in November 1965. — Handels- og Søfartsmuseet, Årbog 1957, p. 8; Kraks Blå Bog 1937, p. 276, s.v. Ekman; Bogvennen 1966, pp. 200–207, the passage on the Tycho volume; Thykier 2003; Helsingør examination 2026; Bruun Rasmussen 186 (1965)
