25Aslakssøn, CortDe natura caeli triplicis libelli tresSiegen, 1597, 4°BNorlind 1970, p. 339 (s.v. Aslakssön, Cort); Zinner no. 3699; VD16 A 3899Cort Aslakssøn (Cunradus Aslacus, Cort Axelsen; Bergen 1564–1624), Tycho’s famulus on Hven 1590–93 and later the first Norwegian professor at Copenhagen. Full title: De natura caeli triplicis libelli tres, quorum I. de caelo aëreo, II. de caelo sidereo, III. de caelo perpetuo, e sacrarum litterarum & praestantium philosophorum thesauris concinnati (Siegen [Sigenae Nassoviorum]: Christoph Rab, Christophorus Corvinus, 1597). VD16 A 3899; Zinner 3699; [7] leaves and 214 pages, octavo (VD16; the ‘4°’ at Norlind, p. 339, is an error). Written and printed while Aslakssøn was abroad as tutor to Otto Brahe (matriculated at Basel 1596/97). The book is dedicated to Tycho (Norlind, p. 339; Store norske leksikon), whence Norlind’s inference that it ‘har väl alltså befunnit sig i Tychos bibliotek’. The ownership rests on that inference alone: no copy with Tycho’s marks and no presentation copy is recorded in the inventories of Prandtl, Kleinschnitzová, Šolc or Mortensen, or in the trade. In the preface Aslakssøn declares for Copernicus, a position Tycho rejected, so that the dedication stands in tension with the book’s own position. The work is a ‘Mosaic physics’, a synthesis of astronomy and scripture on the three heavens (elemental or aerial, astronomical or sidereal, eternal or perpetual); Comenius later listed Aslakssøn among the Mosaic physicists who preceded him. Book III, De caelo perpetuo, was translated into English as ‘The Description of Heaven … Composed in Latine by Cunradus Aslachus, and conuerted into English by Raph [Ralph] Iennings’ (London 1623; EEBO A22193). Copies of the edition survive in Munich (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek), Vienna (Österreichische Nationalbibliothek) and Copenhagen (Royal Library); none is Tycho’s. The dedicatory epistle, read in the Munich copy, is addressed ‘Tychoni Brahe de Knudstrup, fundatori Arcis Uraniburgicae … sideralis scientiae scrutatori, instauratori, conservatori unico’, dated Basel, 16 November 1596, and signed ‘Cunradus Aslachus Bergensis’; it hails Tycho as ‘indefessus Caeli scrutator & Atlas’ and pays eyewitness homage to his celestial globe and astronomical instruments.

113Hagecius, Thaddaeus (Hájek)Apodixis physica et mathematica de cometisGörlitz, 1581, 4°BNorlind 1970, p. 348 (s.v. Hagecius, Thaddæus); Zinner no. 2979 (after Norlind); Kleinschnitzová 1933A copy was sent to Tycho by Scultetus in Görlitz (Tycho to Hagecius, October 1581, Opera omnia VII, 64). Tycho discusses the work in his book on the comet (Opera omnia IV, 293–295). No copy with Tycho’s marks has been identified, and the work belongs to the Hagecius gap: Tycho owned at least four works of Hagecius, each documented by a letter, and not one survives in the Clementinum. Kleinschnitzová (1933, p. 78) remarked on the absence: among the Clementinum Tychoniana there is not a single work of Hagecius, though his long friendship with Tycho and Norlind’s list make it likely that Tycho had many if not all of his works, and none of the Clementinum’s Hagecius copies carries a provenance note pointing to him. Kleinschnitzová (1933, p. 80) names the mechanism by which such a copy could lose its provenance: composite volumes from Tycho’s library were broken up and rebound with pieces of other provenance (5 G 42, 14 J 164, 19 G 20, XII A 12), and Richard Kukula, director of the university library 1897–1918, had old Sammelbände dissolved as a matter of principle, among them, to judge by its modern binding, the Scultetus of 14 F 284. Whether Tycho’s Hagecius copies were lost or survive unrecognised remains an open question. The National Library of the Czech Republic holds three copies of the Görlitz 1581 edition — in a Luther convolute, in a convolute from the Jesuit college at Litoměřice, and as the host volume (record 000030653) of the Hagecius composite owned by the Jesuit college at Chomutov — and none bears a Tycho mark.
